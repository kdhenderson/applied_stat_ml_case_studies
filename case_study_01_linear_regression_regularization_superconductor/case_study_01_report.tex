\section{Introduction}       % Produces section heading.  Lower-level
                             % sections are begun with similar
                             % \subsection and \subsubsection commands.

This study aims to identify important properties of superconductors, materials that give little or no resistance to electrical current, and to predict the critical temperature at which their superconducting properties operate.

The Superconductivity Dataset is a University of California at Irvine Machine Learning Repository dataset. It contains 21,263 entries. The dataset contains two files, one containing material properties, which are numeric features, and the other containing the chemical formula encoded by a vectorized format, with each element represented as a feature and the value being the number of atoms. The material properties file has 81 features plus the target, and the formula file has 86 element-count features. The target variable is the \emph{superconducting critical temperature} in Kelvin. There is no missing data.

A linear regression model is chosen for several reasons. Primarily, it is an appropriate choice with the goal of predicting a continuous variable and having the ability to identify and explain the features most strongly associated with the critical temperature. Secondarily, a linear model is a good choice for computational efficiency.

The target variable in a linear regression model is a linear combination of the input features.
\[ y = m_0x_0 + m_1x_1 + \cdots + m_nx_n \text{ where } x_0 = 1 \text{ and } m_0 \text{ is the intercept.} \]
Penalized linear regression models with different types of regularization are used as a tool to prevent overfitting. Penalty terms are added to the base loss function:
\[ MSE = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(Y_i - \sum_{j=0}^{k} m_j x_{ij}\right)^2 \]
Here I compare LASSO, which uses a first-order L1 penalty term \( \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{k} |m_j| \) to Ridge regression with its second-order L2 penalty term \( \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{k} m_j^2 \) (the intercept \( m_0 \) is not penalized). For interpretability of error in the model and to get a sense of its usefulness and practicality, I will also compute the root mean squared error (RMSE) from the chosen loss metric, mean squared error (MSE). In this report, \(\lambda\) is the alpha parameter in scikit-learn. scikit-learn scales the penalty differently for Lasso and Ridge, so \(\lambda\) values are not comparable between the two models.

\section{Data}
Many of the features in this dataset are different statistical metrics of the same general property. For example, \texttt{FusionHeat} the heat required to change a solid to a liquid, is represented by 10 features: mean, weighted mean, geometric mean, weighted geometric mean, entropy, weighted entropy, range, weighted range, standard deviation and weighted standard deviation. These likely are necessary to describe the material based on the different atoms in its composition. Generally, the middle fifty percent of all the features are roughly symmetric. Some categories of features have outliers, more high than low, and some are skewed, especially in their outer range. Boxplots of fusion heat (see Figure \ref{fig:fusionheat_boxplots}) and valence (see Figure \ref{fig:valence_boxplots}) are representative of variables with, respectively, smaller and larger ranges, greater and lesser skewness, and more and fewer outliers.

Though some of the features have outliers, more commonly high ones, none seem to be unusually abnormal or suggest an error in the data. Given that, and the fairly normal distribution of the data, particularly the reasonable symmetry within the interquartile ranges (IQR), I choose to stick with \texttt{StandardScaler}. I scale only the material property features. I leave the element counts unscaled. They are already in the same units (number of atoms in the formula), and most of their values are zero, so z-scoring them would turn the few large counts into extreme values.

\section{Modeling}
Five-fold cross validation with a random shuffling of the data was used for hyperparameter tuning. Scaling was included in a pipeline with each model, so the scaler was refit on each training fold and the held-out fold never influenced it. The MSE was calculated on each held-out fold and averaged across the five folds; variability is reported as the sample standard deviation (\(n-1\)) of the five fold MSEs. A manual grid search of the penalty term \(\lambda \) was performed for both LASSO and Ridge models, starting with a broad range of values to identify a region likely to optimize the loss. Using the lowest MSE, the best \(\lambda \) for each model was chosen from a second, narrower search. For LASSO, the \(\lambda \) values searched ranged from 0.05 to 10, focusing on smaller increments under 1, while for Ridge the values ranged from \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{4}\) increasing by orders of magnitude with finer increments toward the upper end of that range.
I also chose to tune an ElasticNet model as a comparison to the pure L1 or L2 regularization of the LASSO or Ridge models respectively. For ElasticNet a grid search was conducted for both the penalty term, \(\lambda \), with values in the range from  \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{3}\), and for the L1 mixing term from 0.01 to 0.9, followed by a narrower search of \(\lambda\) from 0.02 to 0.5 and the mixing term from 0.01 to 0.1.

The features with the largest coefficients in the LASSO model are listed in Table~\ref{table:lasso_coefficients}. I use the size of a coefficient (ignoring its sign) as the measure of feature importance: a larger coefficient means a larger change in predicted critical temperature for the same change in that feature, holding the other features fixed. The sign shows the direction of the association. The coefficients are not all on the same scale: property features were standardized, so their coefficients are the change in predicted critical temperature (K) per one standard deviation, while element coefficients are the change per atom of that element in the formula. Rankings across the two groups should be read with that in mind. Many features are also related measures of the same property, so individual rankings should be interpreted with caution. At this penalty, LASSO set 126 of the 167 coefficients to exactly zero (9 of these are elements that never appear in the data), keeping 41 features. Among the elements, Barium, Bismuth, and Calcium are the most important. Unsurprisingly, several of the metrics of thermal conductivity are important in predicting the critical temperature of a material. The range of the atomic mass, and metrics of valence and electron affinity are also important features.

\section{Conclusion}

All three models which used regularization, Lasso, Ridge, and ElasticNet, performed about the same, with mean cross-validated MSEs within about 1 of each other and fold standard deviations of 18 to 34. There was little difference in computational efficiency with the exception of ElasticNet, which required tuning an additional parameter. Additionally, the optimum L1 ratio for ElasticNet was 0.09, meaning its penalty is about 91\% L2 and 9\% L1, so it behaves mostly like Ridge. The LASSO model had a very slightly lower mean MSE and standard deviation, but the differences are small relative to the fold-to-fold variation. The RMSEs of each regularization model, reflecting error in the original units, were all 18 Kelvin. In contrast, the unregularized linear regression model had a much higher error, an RMSE of 26 K, and larger variability than the other models.

The trend and fan shape in the residual plots indicate potential heteroscedasticity in the errors. Although the log transformation did not eliminate the skewness in the target variable distribution, it may be worth revisiting the modeling process using the log-transformed variable to try to improve the variance in the residuals and better meet the assumptions of the model.

The LASSO and Ridge models were consistent in which features had the largest coefficients and in the signs of those coefficients. Barium (per atom) and the weighted mean of thermal conductivity (per standard deviation) were the most important features in both models. These are associations with critical temperature, not evidence that these features determine it.
